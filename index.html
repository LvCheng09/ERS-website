From Events to Reasoning

Why we are exploring a different way to organize AI reasoning

Cheng Lyu · University of Sydney   ·   Xueqi Wang

A research essay  ·  October 2026

arXiv paper· GitHub· Research notebook

What are we actually trying to solve?

AI can now answer increasingly difficult questions. It can read a financial report, outline a solution, call a calculator, search for additional information, and return a conclusion that is fluent and convincing. Sometimes the result is genuinely impressive. Yet one part of this process has continued to bother me: when an AI says, “therefore, this is the answer,” what gives us confidence that every step leading to it was justified?

Imagine asking an AI to assess a company's financial position. You tell it that inventory turnover has slowed, customer demand is unpredictable, and the company is considering holding more safety stock. The system may quickly produce a familiar analysis: excess inventory ties up capital, tied-up capital may put pressure on operating cash flow, and the company should therefore be cautious about replenishment. The paragraph sounds reasonable. But a great deal has been left unresolved. How much did inventory turnover slow? Over what period did demand fluctuate? Are there outstanding purchase commitments? Can existing stock be converted into cash quickly? Are we describing a possible risk, or claiming that a particular loss has already occurred?

On a small problem, a human reviewer can sometimes catch these omissions. When an answer requires dozens of dependent steps across several domains, and new facts can arrive at any time, unsupported assumptions become much harder to notice. Even a numerically correct final answer does not establish that the right data, periods, definitions, or permissions were used along the way. And if one earlier fact later turns out to be wrong, many systems have no direct way to repair only the reasoning affected by that fact; they generate the answer again.

I am interested in a reasoning process that can remain in operation as a problem evolves. It should know which facts it is using, where they came from, why an operation is allowed, what new claim the operation produced, and which later conclusions depend on it. When circumstances change, it should be able to identify what needs checking again. When evidence is missing, it should be able to stop and say what is missing instead of making an attractive guess.

This is the starting point of the Event Reasoning System (ERS): to give reasoning a structure that can be inspected, executed, retained, and revised. Language models remain essential, especially for understanding open-ended inputs. But the organization of the reasoning itself deserves a form more explicit than another passage of natural-language explanation.

What existing approaches have taught us

Several research directions have already moved AI reasoning a long way forward. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) encourages a model to articulate intermediate steps. ReAct connects reasoning with actions and observations. Tree of Thoughts (ToT) explores and compares alternative branches rather than following only one continuation. Breaking a task into parts, using tools, considering different routes, and gathering missing information are valuable capabilities that any serious reasoning architecture should learn from.

Still, an intermediate “step” written in language is itself a generated statement. It may express a valid inference, but it may also leave out a crucial premise. We could ask another model to inspect it, and such checks may be useful. Yet the underlying question remains: what authorizes the check? Is it another judgment about a plausible sentence, or a condition grounded in the facts and constraints available for this specific task? Generative reasoning continues to improve. For problems involving precise evidence, identity, time, and numerical scope, the validity of the process deserves separate attention.

Other approaches hand part of the job to programs or formal solvers. PAL makes use of executable programs; Logic-LM connects language models to symbolic reasoning. Classical planning developed explicit operators and preconditions decades ago, while systems such as Soar explored working memory, production rules, and dynamically generated subgoals. These are important predecessors, not obstacles to our work. They demonstrate why an operation should have conditions, why a large task may need to be decomposed, and why execution should change the state on which later decisions are made.

Where formal or executable approaches are especially strong is in operating on a representation that is already precise. The difficulty is that real problems rarely arrive as clean symbolic statements. They arrive as financial reports, conversations, documents, images, and incomplete observations. Before deciding what may be done, a system must work out what the input actually says. Moving from flexible, sometimes ambiguous meaning to a task-specific executable state is difficult in its own right.

World models provide another influential direction. Broadly, they try to learn internal representations of an environment and how those representations change, so that an agent can predict consequences and plan. Research including Dreamer and the JEPA family explores aspects of representation, prediction, and learning. One idea we find especially compelling is that complex situations can be understood through the properties of things, the relations among them, and the changes that occur over time.

That led us to a more specific question: if AI can learn and recognize features and relationships from open-ended information, can those representations enter an explicit reasoning process in which checkable conditions determine which operations are actually allowed? Predicting that something is likely to happen and establishing that an inference is admissible under current evidence are different tasks. In some applications they can work together; when an answer must be auditable, the second task becomes particularly important.

ERS builds on these lines of work by giving different responsibilities to different parts of a system. A semantic model interprets open inputs. Explicit events preserve the state of the current problem. Reusable rules perform local operations under declared conditions. Control rules organize the route toward a goal using both the current state and feedback from execution. Rule-based execution, hierarchical planning, and dependency tracking all have established precedents. Our research concerns how they can meet at a common event-state interface, and how a semantically nominated candidate becomes a legitimately admitted operation.

For the formal positioning, see the paper's background and related-work discussion. For the practical separation of modules, see the technical report.

Why begin with features?

My thinking started from a simple observation: we usually recognize something through the features it exhibits and the relationships it has with other things.

Take an ordinary glass. We can describe it as transparent, made of glass, filled with water, and resting on a table. Some of these statements describe the object itself; others describe how it relates to something else. If someone picks it up, its material may remain unchanged while its relationship to the table changes. If it breaks, its state of being intact changes. A useful description of the glass therefore has to include properties, relations, and changes—not merely a label saying “glass.”

Now consider something abstract, like a company. We cannot see “the company” as one physical object in the way we see a glass. We describe it through employees, contracts, transactions, financial indicators, organizational relationships, and events occurring during particular periods. If we say inventory has affected the company's cash flow, we are already referring to a collection of properties and relationships: whose inventory, in which period, how quickly it turns over, how it ties up capital, and under what conditions that can affect cash flow.

Even the short statement “Apple increased by 10%” reveals the problem. Does Apple mean a company or a fruit crop? Increased what: revenue, shipment volume, market price, weight, or production? Without the surrounding entity, attribute, relation, and time, a system does not really know what this sentence describes. Similarity between the words “Apple” and “increase” is not enough.

From this comes a modeling hypothesis: within the scope and precision of a particular task, relevant objects and changes can often be described using features and relations. This is a way to organize observable information. It is not a proof that the entire universe can be exhaustively represented by one fixed set of features. Measurements may be noisy; observations may be incomplete; contextual knowledge may be missing; and some relevant properties may not yet have been discovered. The question we can investigate is narrower and more useful: can we preserve enough features and relationships to give subsequent reasoning clear objects, states, and grounds?

This perspective also suggests a possible common interface for different input modalities. An image might tell us that a glass is to the left of a book; a sentence might tell us that it contains hot water; a sensor might provide a temperature. These observations come from different sources and have different uncertainties, but they may refer to the same object. The hard work lies in establishing that alignment correctly while retaining exact numerical values, identities, and sources. Our currently released ERS demo has a text-based semantic frontend; broader multimodal grounding remains research and implementation work.

In ERS, we divide the semantic description into three interpretable aspects: what core concept is involved (core), what relationship is being expressed (relation), and what property or qualification it carries (nature). Alongside these representations, we preserve exact quantities, units, entity identities, reporting periods, and source references. The distinction matters because similar meaning helps us find potentially relevant information; it cannot, on its own, establish that the information is authorized for the calculation or inference at hand.

Think of semantic features as a flexible index that can direct us toward the right concepts. Explicit identifiers, measurements, dates, and evidence conditions are what make a particular use of those concepts eligible. We need both: flexible understanding to encounter an open world, and explicit checks to keep operations connected to the current task.

A formal description of this assumption appears in the paper's feature-based representation section. The technical report's semantic-representation section explains how the released code separates semantic vectors from exact fields.

From features to events, and from events to reasoning

Features help describe an object, but most interesting questions involve more than one object. There are multiple times, several observations, events that have occurred, events that may occur, and relationships among them. We therefore organize relevant features into an Event.

An Event does not have to be a dramatic occurrence. It may represent a sale, an observation, a financial state during a reporting period, or an intermediate result that has been accepted after calculation. It brings together the objects, features, relations, time, evidence, and goal involved in the current problem. Related events can then be connected into an Event Graph, which evolves as reasoning proceeds.

Suppose we want to know whether a company facing slower inventory turnover may experience pressure on operating cash flow. First we need to establish which company, which period, which observation supports the claim that turnover slowed, and what additional conditions connect inventory to capital being tied up. Only then can we examine whether those conditions support a further inference about cash flow. Where the evidence supports merely a possible exposure, that uncertainty should remain visible; a possible risk is not automatically an observed loss.

As reasoning proceeds, each admitted inference can produce an intermediate result that becomes available to later operations. These results remain associated with their supporting facts and operations. The result is a history of state changes that we can follow: where the process started, what it tried, what it established, and what remains unresolved.

An Event Graph, however, does not solve a problem by itself. We still need to specify what an operation requires and what it is allowed to produce. This is the role of a Rule.

A Rule may be as ordinary as adding two compatible numbers, averaging opening and closing balances that belong to the same permitted accounting scope, or computing a ratio once the numerator, denominator, and time convention are authorized. Some Rules produce new measurements; others express relationships among events. Each describes a local capability: what inputs it needs, the conditions under which it applies, and the effect it can produce. The same Rule can be used on different objects and periods as long as those bindings satisfy its conditions.

This resembles how we solve unfamiliar problems with familiar operations. Once we have learned addition, subtraction, comparison, or classification, we do not need to memorize a separate complete solution for every possible set of numbers. We reuse smaller capabilities with new inputs and new combinations. ERS investigates how this kind of reuse can extend to more complex event and relation reasoning.

But having many Rules does not mean we should execute all of them. Several operations may be legal in the same state, and only some of them may bring us closer to our goal. Others consume resources without helping. This is why we introduce Control: rules and state that organize which subgoal to pursue, which admissible path to try next, and when the current strategy should change. Control takes account of the goal, available facts, missing premises, feedback, and computational budget.

The distinction is straightforward. A lower-level execution Rule performs a local operation under conditions. Higher-level Control organizes those operations. In the theoretical design, a Control Rule may invoke a more specialized Control Rule, leading to a task-dependent hierarchy rather than a permanently fixed number of levels. More hierarchy is not automatically better; it is useful only when the reduction in wasted exploration justifies its own organizational cost.

Imagine that the chosen path is to calculate an average, form a ratio, and convert the ratio into days. If the averaging step lacks a required input, the system should find out whether its existing facts and Knowledge can support a legitimate completion, or whether another permitted route exists. It must not invent a balance just to keep going. This failure is information for Control: which premise was missing, which attempts have failed, and whether the current strategy remains promising.

Knowledge and Memory play different supporting roles. Knowledge supplies reusable definitions and relations, potentially allowing a semantic property to be completed when current evidence actually supports it. Memory can offer clues about paths that were useful in earlier tasks. Neither supplies a missing measurement merely because the task would be easier with one, and an answer obtained yesterday is not evidence that the same answer holds today. In the released demo, Memory is a runtime guidance interface, not an already-trained Auto-Learn capability.

This brings the architecture together: a semantic model interprets expression; features and relations are organized into Events; Events preserve the current factual state; local Rules perform admitted transformations; and Control uses goals and feedback to compose and revise the path. A finite set of capabilities may thus form paths that were never stored as complete solutions, provided the necessary Rules, facts, and search budget are available.

The theoretical formulation is in the paper's architecture and finite-rule composition sections. The architecture report maps the corresponding released components to their interfaces and execution loop.

A small financial example with a surprisingly important detail

Consider a company whose Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) we want to calculate for FY2025. We are given an opening accounts receivable balance of 40, a closing balance of 60, FY2025 revenue of 500, and an additional revenue figure of 450 for FY2024. The task explicitly authorizes using the average of opening and closing receivables, the indicated revenue basis, and a 365-day convention. These are explanatory values from the paper's appendix; the authorization matters because “revenue” is not universally interchangeable with net credit sales.

The arithmetic is easy. Average receivables is (40 + 60) / 2 = 50. The ratio is 50 / 500 = 0.1. Multiplying by 365 gives 36.5 days. A language model that knows the financial formula could produce this number in seconds.

But notice that we deliberately included a second revenue value: 450 from FY2024. It refers to the same company, has the same broad semantic category, and may look very relevant to a semantic search system. If a system simply selects a similar-looking number, it may use the wrong period. A calculator would still return a clean, precise result—just one based on an invalid input.

ERS addresses this by preserving each quantity with its identity, value, reporting period, and evidence context. Semantic similarity can identify both revenue figures as candidates. The Rule's declared conditions then require the denominator to match the authorized period and measurement basis. The FY2024 value is excluded from this binding; the FY2025 value is eligible. We call this nomination–admission separation: semantic representation proposes candidates, while explicit conditions determine which candidate may participate in an actual operation.

Control can then organize the goal into dependent operations: compute average receivables, compute the ratio using the authorized denominator, and apply the day convention. Each admitted intermediate result can become an input to the next operation. The final 36.5 days has a supporting path: two receivables observations, an eligible revenue figure, a time convention, and the Rules that connected them.

The benefit of preserving these relationships becomes clearer when a fact changes. Suppose the FY2025 revenue is corrected from 500 to 625. Average receivables remains 50 because neither opening nor closing receivables changed. The new ratio is 50 / 625 = 0.08, so the corrected DSO is 29.2 days. Under the paper's dependency-maintenance design, the system can identify the calculations that depend on the corrected revenue while preserving intermediate conclusions whose support is unaffected. This is a mechanism we want to generalize, rather than a claim that the released v0.1 demo already maintains every possible dependency.

The lesson goes beyond financial arithmetic. Producing a number is relatively easy; knowing why the number is valid, when it is valid, and what would make it invalid is a different reasoning problem.

The paper's Appendix A presents the fuller DSO mechanism and figure. The technical report's DSO walkthrough explains the released pipeline. The paper's generalized dependency revision and the public demo's narrower source and output checks should not be conflated.

What difference do we hope this makes?

First, we want reasoning operations to have explicit eligibility. A retrieved passage, a close semantic match, or a familiar-looking variable is not yet permission to use it. Identity, period, unit, relation, and the evidence admitted by the task may still need to be checked. This can prevent a class of failures where every sentence reads smoothly but some intermediate step quietly used an inapplicable fact. The guarantee is conditional, however: it depends on the correctness of the interpreted input and the adequacy of the declared Rule. If the initial representation is wrong or an important constraint was omitted, a structural checker cannot magically discover every error.

Second, we want reusable local capabilities to form new paths. Storing a complete solution for every future problem is an unrealistic strategy. Reusing smaller operations and rebinding them to new facts may make a system more adaptable. But the number of possible combinations is not the same as the number of solvable tasks. The scope of the Rule library, the quality of observations, compatibility of operations, and the resources available for search all matter.

Third, we want the reasoning process to have adjustable organization. Difficult problems contain subgoals and branches that sometimes lead nowhere. Control should be able to use successes, refusals, missing premises, and changes of state to reorganize the next step. The research ambition is for this organization to depend on the task: simple problems should not need extravagant planning, while complex problems may benefit from nested subgoals and feedback that stays local where possible. Planning itself consumes resources, so improvements in efficiency need to be measured end to end rather than assumed from fewer executed domain Rules.

Fourth, we want conclusions to preserve their grounds. A result may have more than one independent support path. Losing one source of evidence does not necessarily invalidate every conclusion supported through another path. A representation of these dependencies can make revision more targeted. It also forces us to distinguish missing evidence, withdrawn evidence, and evidence that actually contradicts a claim; these are different informational states, and “not proven” is not the same as “disproven.”

Finally, we want definitions, experience, and executable capability to remain distinguishable. Knowledge maintains reusable conceptual definitions. Memory can prioritize an exploration path learned from experience. A Rule specifies what an operation may do; Control organizes when and why to try it. Changes to a long-term capability library are different from temporary updates inside one reasoning episode. The released demo does not include autonomous Rule learning or post-training.

This also suggests a complementary relationship with world models. A world model may estimate how an environment could evolve; ERS may check whether a given inference or action is admissible under the present task's explicit conditions and traceable supports. Predictions could enter an Event Graph as tentative information, carrying source and uncertainty rather than being silently promoted to fact. How to build and evaluate this interface is a research question. We are not claiming that all world models lack planning, nor that they cannot already incorporate external validators.

Where the work stands, and what we still suspect

We have released the core ideas of ERS in a research preprint and a runnable open-source inference demo. The paper develops feature-based representation, applicability checks, dynamic Control, finite Rule composition, evidence dependencies, and targeted revision as a theoretical architecture accompanied by structured-event experiments. The public v0.1 repository demonstrates a text semantic frontend, Event Graph boundary, retrieval, Matcher, bounded Control delegation and feedback, Executor, Calculator, and output acceptance, centered on finance and inventory/supply-chain cases.

The paper reports dependent chains up to twelve steps and twelve targeted frozen-rule checks: nine supported derivations and three justified refusals. It also reports six targeted fact-change checks. In a development subset of fourteen tasks solved correctly under both compared configurations, the dynamic-subgoal configuration recorded 51.6% less reasoning wall-clock time than single-level global control. These measurements have important boundaries. The experiments use author-constructed structured inputs; control budgets have not been established as fully matched; and none of these numbers is an end-to-end performance claim for the released public demo. We regard these results as a starting point for stronger evidence, not as a promise of open-world generality.

Several unanswered questions are now central to the work. If the starting Event Graph is generated from language or images rather than constructed by the experimenter, how do we measure errors introduced by semantic interpretation? When the Rule network becomes large and many legal paths compete, how should a limited search budget be allocated? When the world changes, can we implement full dependency and independent-support maintenance, and show precisely when local revision is cheaper than recomputation? If the current Rule library cannot express a needed operation, how can new capabilities be acquired, validated, retained, and safely revised?

There are also longer-term hypotheses that I want to make visible while they are still hypotheses.

One concerns local processing and global explicitness. Perhaps an intelligent architecture can use processing units that carry out bounded local functions—some continuous, approximate, or learnable—while connecting those units through more explicit conditions at the level of the reasoning path. Biological neural systems offer a suggestive analogy for thinking about local computation and larger-scale organization. An analogy is motivation to investigate a design; it is not evidence that ERS has reproduced the biological mechanism or that the architecture is therefore correct.

Another concerns how a system should train and learn. What sequence of examples, observations, and optimization choices helps a model improve as efficiently as possible? Could the next useful piece of training data be selected in light of current Rule coverage, uncertainty, expected information gain, and computational cost? Such work would involve proposing and validating new Rules, resolving conflicts, and maintaining a capability library over time. Those mechanisms are research directions; they are not part of the released v0.1 inference demo.

A third concerns multimodal grounding. We are exploring how inputs from language, images, audio, and other sources might enter compatible Event interfaces while retaining exact facts, provenance, and uncertainty. If that interface works, the same general principles of local Rules, compositional Control, and evidential acceptance might be useful in more domains. Whether this extends to genuinely open-world tasks, how new Rules would be learned, and what resources it would require are questions for future experiments, not settled conclusions.

The next meaningful step is to turn these ideas into reproducible experiments, explicit failure criteria, and evidence that other researchers can inspect independently. We plan to record experiments, implementation progress, unexpected failures, and new conjectures in our Research Notebook. The paper and technical report can then be updated when stronger results are available.

We welcome researchers and developers who would like to challenge these ideas, reproduce them, or work on the unanswered questions. We would also welcome support for the practical costs of independent research, especially hardware, compute, and data generation. Details and project activity are available through our open-source repository.

For us, ERS is an exploration of how intelligent systems might organize reasoning: let information from an open world enter describable Event states; let a finite set of reusable abilities form new admissible paths; let actual execution revise the direction of Control; and keep each accepted conclusion connected to its grounds. There is still a great deal to test. Those unproven boundaries are an essential part of what we want to investigate next.
